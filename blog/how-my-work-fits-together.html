How my work fits together

October 15, 2026

The question

I study human-inspired and human-like learning in machine learning models. Humans and models overlap in some ways and differ in others. Sometimes they share a property but express it differently. My working belief is that many intuitions about how people learn can be formalized in models. I hope to find which intuitions transfer, understand why others don’t, and turn the ones that do into learning methods. Many won’t transfer, and shouldn’t be expected to, so I try to be careful about which ones to build on.

I come at this from two sides:

  1. A model’s own properties. Some properties come from a model’s inductive biases, and they can be put to use directly.
  2. Properties inspired by humans. Some of the ways people learn can be formalized and built into models.

Most of this work is in continual learning (when and how a model should take in new information without forgetting) and meta-learning (how a model can get more out of the data it has).

A model’s own properties

Continual learning in diffusion models. The empirical Fisher of a diffusion model is rank-1 in low-SNR regimes. That makes a rank-1 EWC penalty possible, and combined with replay it more than halves forgetting on ImageNet-1k relative to replay and diagonal-EWC baselines. (ICLR 2026)

Continual learning as meta-learning. The Fisher penalty keeps each update inside a trust region, so we call the method trust region continual learning. Under local approximations, it is equivalent to one-step MAML, with replay as the query and the Fisher penalty as the support. In that sense it implicitly meta-learns previous tasks, and the effect shows up directly: the model re-learns earlier tasks faster than with other methods. (NeurIPS 2026)

Properties inspired by humans

Multi-role reinforcement learning. Humans get far more out of very little data than LLMs do. Part of that is inductive bias; part is how people process the data. They learn the same material in more than one way, by comparing problems, identifying subgoals, or explaining, and they abstract across examples. Multi-role RL trains one language model in several such roles over the same examples. Roles that never answer a question directly still improve problem solving. This points to transfer across tasks within LLMs, and to more training signal in a fixed dataset than standard RL with verifiable rewards extracts. Learning to abstract improves generalization, looking across several examples at once gives more signal than seeing one at a time, and learning the same material from different views helps even when those views are learned separately. (Cross-Task Transfer via Multi-Role Reinforcement Learning, preprint.)

Test-time compositional generalization via prototype discovery. Compositional generalization is producing new configurations from familiar parts. Humans do this freely, combining concepts they already know into things they have never seen. We find a way to express this in diffusion models, so that a pretrained model can compose what it already knows at test time. (Preprint)
